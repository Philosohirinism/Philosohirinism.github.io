English transcript
  1. 0:01 Previously: both repairs failed—the picture to be repaired was already gone from the frame.
  2. 0:07 This time: the frame is empty, but a new group arrives onstage.
  3. 0:15 Episode 6: Dissenters Resemble a Small State
  4. 0:21 Imagine a national theater: a big stage, a big flag, and an audience full of “us.”
  5. 0:29 As earlier episodes explained, this play's meaning has dispersed. The audience leaves one by one. The big flag still hangs, but no one watches.
  6. 0:37 Yet the desire for a “we,” for someone to represent us, has not dispersed with it.
  7. 0:44 Author's thesis, chapter 4 introduction: after the symbolic collapse of the nation–state–sovereignty triad, the desire for collective identity and representational legitimacy remains. (Paraphrase)
  8. 0:53 Then a group brings little flags and a little lectern of its own, and starts performing again in front of the empty stage.
  9. 1:00 They do not mount the state's high platform. They connect horizontally below it, gathering through shared marginalization, emotional bonds, and the very fact of being excluded.
  10. 1:12 Transversal dissent: authority comes from shared marginalization, emotional solidarity, and the visibility of “being excluded,” rather than institutional embeddedness.
  11. 1:23 Author's thesis §4.1, drawing on Bleiker's (2000) concept of “transversal dissent.” (Paraphrase)
  12. 1:28 Pause: compare the new stage with the old. Can you find three similarities?
  13. 1:39 Flags, a lectern, and a line separating “us” from “them.” The stage is smaller; the grammar is unchanged.
  14. 1:46 Author's thesis §4.1: these groups differ organizationally from the state, yet their discursive strategies often imitate the nation-state, building a symbolic “we” through collective suffering, mythic memory, or exclusionary binaries (the thesis cites Brown, 2001; Agathangelou & Ling, 2004). (Paraphrase)
  15. 1:56 The author calls this a Transient Legitimacy Polity.
  16. 2:03 Its legitimacy comes neither from stable institutions nor from a coherent history, but from its performative identification with “the oppressed” and “the marginalized.”
  17. 2:14 It depends on the urgency of “speaking out now,” rather than a vision of “what comes next.” In a phrase: legitimacy without legacy.
  18. 2:23 Author's thesis §4.1: “It is legitimacy without legacy.” Its legitimacy comes from “the urgency of voice, not the durability of vision.”
  19. 2:30 Why does the new stage look like the old one?
  20. 2:34 Because resistance rarely stands outside power. It often grows within it, rewriting the old ways of speaking rather than replacing them.
  21. 2:44 Author's thesis §4.1, citing Butler (1997): resistance rarely lies outside the power structures it challenges. It is often embedded in them, resignifying dominant discourse rather than directly replacing it. (Paraphrase)
  22. 2:52 A caveat: a Transient Legitimacy Polity is a symptom, not an accusation. The suffering of those onstage is real, and so is their exclusion.
  23. 2:59 It is neither pure liberation nor complete co-optation. It shows only that, beyond the old language, we have no other language yet.
  24. 3:09 Author's thesis §4.1: a Transient Legitimacy Polity is not just a new political entity but a symptom—“neither purely emancipatory nor fully co-opted.”
