English transcript
  1. 0:01 Previously: to have an “us,” a line must first be drawn; on the other side, there must always be a “them.”
  2. 0:07 This time: five forces are loosening the whole production line.
  3. 0:15 Episode 4: Five Forces That Loosen the Line
  4. 0:22 Transnational identities
  5. 0:25 Suppose a child holds two passports. When the two countries meet at the World Cup, she holds two little flags, unsure whom to cheer for.
  6. 0:34 “Which country are you from?” no longer has just one answer. The boundaries of “us” begin to overlap.
  7. 0:40 Author's thesis §3.1: dual nationality, diasporic identification, and transnational solidarity weaken the exclusivity of national belonging (citing Vertovec, 2009; Levitt & Glick Schiller, 2004). (Paraphrase)
  8. 0:46 Technological deterritorialization
  9. 0:50 She lives here, but at three in the morning she meets with a company in another country. A platform pays her wages and sets the rules; her data sits on servers elsewhere.
  10. 1:01 State borders remain, but more and more of her daily life is governed by actors other than the state.
  11. 1:07 Author's thesis §3.1: digital technologies let flows of capital, information, and power bypass states; platform protocols govern “digital subjects” (citing Castells, 1996; Sassen, 2006). (Paraphrase)
  12. 1:14 Symbolic fragmentation
  13. 1:17 Same country, same day. One person sees a solemn flag raising, another a satirical joke, and a third an angry long post.
  14. 1:27 People no longer share the same story. The grand narrative of “us” begins to lose their belief.
  15. 1:34 Author's thesis §3.1: Lyotard's (1984) “incredulity toward metanarratives”; Butler's (1990) identity performances can fail, be parodied, and be given new meanings. (Paraphrase)
  16. 1:41 Risk governance
  17. 1:44 During the pandemic years, you scanned a code before entering. The gate asked not whether you were “one of us,” but what color your risk was.
  18. 1:53 What is managed is no longer a member of a nation, but a set of risk data to be calculated.
  19. 2:00 Author's thesis §3.1: late-modern governance revolves around risks within systems (citing Beck, 1992); populations are governed as biological-statistical objects (citing Foucault, 2008). (Paraphrase)
  20. 2:07 The disappearance of the Other
  21. 2:10 The Cold War ends. The once unmistakable “enemy” poster on the wall fades. The external “them” grows less clear, while the internal “them” multiplies.
  22. 2:20 “We must exist because they threaten us”—that sentence can no longer find its object.
  23. 2:27 Author's thesis §3.1: internal Others multiply; the external Other becomes internal and hard to identify. The fear-based mechanism of legitimacy fails (citing Said, 1978). (Paraphrase)
  24. 2:34 Count them: how many of these five situations have you experienced?
  25. 2:44 To be clear: coming loose is not breaking apart. Passports are still issued, gates still open, and taxes are still collected.
  26. 2:52 Something else changes: the machine still runs, but no longer carries meaning of its own. The state remains as an institution; as an answer to “who we are,” it starts to drift.
  27. 3:03 Author's thesis §3.1: the state still exists institutionally, but loses its symbolic anchor and floats. (Paraphrase)
  28. 3:10 The passport is still there.
  29. 3:12 Its edges are just beginning to blur.
