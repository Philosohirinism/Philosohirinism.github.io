English transcript
  1. 0:01 Previously: the production line's second station first divides people into “us” and “them.”
  2. 0:06 This time, we question that station: without “them,” would there still be an “us”?
  3. 0:14 Episode 3: No “Us” Without “Them”?
  4. 0:21 Suppose it is nine in the morning at a school sports day. Students from one year group stand in the field, chatting in small groups.
  5. 0:29 The teacher draws lots and gives everyone a headband. Red or blue. Entirely at random.
  6. 0:39 “Red team will win!”
  7. 0:41 “Go, blue team!”
  8. 0:42 Half an hour ago, they were simply classmates. Now his desk mate is on the other side. He scores a basket, and people here start booing.
  9. 0:49 Nothing has changed except a headband. Yet “us” and “them” have already appeared.
  10. 0:59 Choose one: if you were randomly put on the red team, which team would you rather see win?
  11. 1:08 In a famous psychology experiment, strangers were split into two groups by an arbitrary criterion. Even when the grouping meant nothing, they favored “their own group” when allocating rewards.
  12. 1:20 Additional example (not in the thesis): Tajfel, Billig, Bundy & Flament (1971), “Social categorization and intergroup behaviour,” European Journal of Social Psychology 1: 149–177. The minimal group experiment; paraphrased here.
  13. 1:29 The thesis calls this the logic of “us/the Other”: “we” can only be fully constituted in opposition to “them.”
  14. 1:38 Drawing this line does two things at once: it brings some people in and gives a reason to keep others out.
  15. 1:47 Author's thesis §2.3: subjectivity is produced through repeated boundaries (Butler, 1997); the nation as “we” stands against a constructed Other (Said, 1978); political identity always needs opposition, and “we” becomes complete only against “them” (paraphrasing Mouffe, 2005).
  16. 1:57 Back to episode one's three pillars: nation, state, and sovereignty.
  17. 2:03 The thesis argues that this closed loop needs an “Other”: internal, such as minorities; or external, such as an enemy.
  18. 2:11 “They threaten us, so we must exist.” A perceived threat becomes the reason to hold the three pillars together and deem them necessary.
  19. 2:20 Author's thesis §2.2 (citing Walker, 1993): the loop must construct an internal or external “Other” to define and defend the boundaries of “us.” §3.1 sums up the mechanism as “We must exist because the Other threatens us.”
  20. 2:29 A counterexample: not every “we” depends on an enemy. A choir can feel a strong “we” without an opponent—through doing something together.
  21. 2:38 But the nation's “we” is different: binding millions of strangers together makes it hard to do without a “them.”
  22. 2:47 Where can a “we” without enemies come from? We will return to that in the final episode.
  23. 2:56 This episode's reference card (take a screenshot)
  24. 2:59 Us/the Other | Red team and blue team | “We” is constituted through opposition.
  25. 3:02 Boundary | A headband, a white line | Brings people in and keeps others out.
  26. 3:06 The Other justifies the closed loop | “They threaten us” | Threat makes the three pillars seem necessary.
  27. 3:11 Counterexample | A choir | Doing things together can also create a “we.”
  28. 3:18 To have an “us,” a line must first be drawn.
  29. 3:21 And on the other side, there must always be a “them.”

  31. 3:30 How do five forces loosen this production line?
