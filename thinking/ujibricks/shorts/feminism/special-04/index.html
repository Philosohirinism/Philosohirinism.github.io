Who Pays for “Independence”?

Praise for feminine qualities is set beside the labour that supports an apparently independent individual.

English transcript
  1. 0:00 “Women are naturally good at caring. What a blessing.”
  2. 0:03 “Care is noble work. Women's relationality is a source of strength.”
  3. 0:07 Two statements. Opposite judgments.
  4. 0:09 The same premise.
  5. 0:15 Judgments do not change the pay slip.
  6. 0:19 “Better suited to homemaking” is produced by repeatedly assigning that role.
  7. 0:23 But within feminism, one path also led elsewhere.
  8. 0:28 In the mid-to-late 1970s, some US radical feminists turned toward “cultural feminism”:
  9. 0:34 rather than dismantling the gendered division of labor, they affirmed feminine traits: care, connection and motherhood.
  10. 0:41 Here is the problem.
  11. 0:42 If relationality is women's essence, it shares a premise with “women should look after the home.”
  12. 0:49 Tradition says: therefore, it is your duty.
  13. 0:51 This says: therefore, it is your glory.
  14. 0:55 The more motherhood, housework and emotional labor are praised, the more they resemble an unpaid calling.
  15. 1:00 Praise does not change who takes leave.
  16. 1:02 This is not every radical feminist's position. Millett, Firestone and others sought to dismantle precisely this division of labor.
  17. 1:10 Then leave relationships aside. Talk about the individual.
  18. 1:12 Mainstream liberal thought promises an “individual”: self-owning, freely choosing, personally responsible.
  19. 1:19 But when this person has a fever, who takes leave?
  20. 1:22 Who remembers his follow-up appointment? Who picks up the children when he works late?
  21. 1:25 Carole Pateman argues that the social contract's “individual” assumes someone at home carries the burden of his dependency.
  22. 1:31 His independence is built on someone else's care.
  23. 1:36 Women are caught between two paths.
  24. 1:39 Want equality? Become that “individual” and offload care.
  25. 1:43 Onto whom? Often poorer women. More in Episode 6.
  26. 1:47 Want care recognized? Remain in relationships, in the place tradition already assigned you.
  27. 1:52 Pateman calls this the “Wollstonecraft dilemma.”
  28. 1:57 Both paths end alike: someone carries another's dependency and is not counted as “independent.”
  29. 2:04 A psychoanalytic reading, not an empirical finding.
  30. 2:07 For Lacan, no one truly possesses the “phallus.”
  31. 2:11 The masculine position is the fantasy of having it: being whole, lacking nothing, owing no one.
  32. 2:15 The liberal individual resembles an ego pretending it has not been castrated.
  33. 2:21 He does depend on others.
  34. 2:23 He simply refuses to acknowledge it.
  35. 2:26 Those who admit dependence are called “not independent enough.”
  36. 2:31 But the critique must cut both ways.
  37. 2:33 “Western individualism cannot resolve relationships.”
  38. 2:37 That claim is also being used to send women home.
  39. 2:41 Exposing the fantasy of independence does not mean returning to assigned dependency.
  40. 2:46 Nancy Fraser offered a direction: rather than making women live as men do now, let men live as women do now, combining work and care.
  41. 2:55 Treat dependence as a fact of everyone's life, not one gender's calling.
  42. 3:01 So the real question is not whether women should be independent.
  43. 3:04 It is: who pays for someone's “independence”?
  44. 3:10 In the next main episode, we keep asking:
  45. 3:13 Between “she can give birth” and “so she should shoulder all of this,”
  46. 3:17 who left out the argument on our behalf?
