  14. 1:13 This distinction has a long history in philosophy.
  15. 1:17 Object — Fear: a specific threat. Anxiety: no clear object; philosophers call its object ‘nothingness.’
  16. 1:23 Response — Fear: two options, fight or flight. Anxiety: many possibilities, including repetition, freezing, rushing out, or even excitement.
  17. 1:29 Rumelili 2020, pp. 258–259: the existentialist tradition (Kierkegaard, Heidegger, Tillich) distinguishes fear from anxiety: fear concerns a specific object; anxiety has ‘nothingness’ as its object.
  18. 1:34 Kinnvall & Mitzen 2020, p. 241: fear leads to ‘fight or flight’; anxiety lacks a clear object and may elicit many emotions and behaviors.
  19. 1:41 The theologian Tillich distinguished fear of death from anxiety about death.
  20. 1:47 If you fear a car crash, you can fasten your seat belt.
  21. 1:51 But there is no seat belt for ‘one day, I will no longer exist.’
  22. 1:56 Tillich’s distinction, as recounted in Rumelili 2020, p. 259.
  23. 2:01 So people do something strange: they find an object for their anxiety.
  24. 2:09 Because only fear can be confronted, attacked, or endured.
  25. 2:13 Tillich’s view, as recounted in Rumelili 2020, p. 259; p. 267: unlike nothingness, objects can be managed and fought back against.
  26. 2:16 Drawing a wolf feels better than facing the dark.
  27. 2:23 States can do this too.
  28. 2:27 Two countries have argued for decades. Arguing has become their daily routine.
  29. 2:32 When peace finally comes, they become restless instead.
  30. 2:36 Conflict at least gave them a definite object; peace releases the anxiety they had kept at bay.
  31. 2:43 Mitzen 2006, p. 341: even dangerous routines provide ontological security; states may become attached to conflict. Rumelili 2020, p. 268: conflict offers a definite object of fear; prospects of peace may release suppressed anxiety.
  32. 2:48 This does not mean peace is bad. It means people and states seek more than survival.
