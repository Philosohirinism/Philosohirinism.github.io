  1. 0:01 Have you ever looked closely at the first page of your passport?
  2. 0:05 On that page stand the three pillars of the modern state.
  3. 0:12 Episode 1: Three Terms, One Closed Loop
  4. 0:19 Open it. This is a fictional passport, but the format will look familiar.
  5. 0:24 Pause here: find three things on these two pages—a name, an authority, and a request.
  6. 0:35 The name “Country X”; the authority that issued it; and a request addressed to other countries.
  7. 0:44 First, look at “Nationality: Country X.”
  8. 0:48 The national anthem plays before a match. She knows none of the tens of thousands in the stands.
  9. 0:53 Yet she feels they are all part of “us.”
  10. 0:56 Nation: it gives you a “we,” while drawing a line around those outside.
  11. 1:00 Anderson, 1983: a nation is an “imagined community.” Most members never meet, yet share a sense of “we.” (Paraphrase)
  12. 1:07 Next, look at “Issuing authority.”
  13. 1:11 “We” is a feeling. Who you are, and whether you can enter, are settled through counters, stamps, gates, and borders.
  14. 1:18 State: it turns “we” into rules, documents, and procedures.
  15. 1:23 Weber, 1919 (Gerth & Mills's 1946 English translation): a state is a human community that successfully monopolizes the legitimate use of force within a territory. (Paraphrase)
  16. 1:29 Finally, the request addressed to other countries.
  17. 1:33 Sovereignty: having the final say within this territory.
  18. 1:38 Normally, everyone follows the rules. When the rules fail, who can say, “The rules are now suspended”?
  19. 1:44 Schmitt: “Sovereign is he who decides on the exception.” (Political Theology, Schwab's English translation, 2005, p. 5)
  20. 1:48 Having the final say at home is not enough. The request addresses other countries: please recognize this passport, and this state, as real.
  21. 1:56 Real example: the first page of a British passport asks, in the King's name, that “all those whom it may concern” let the bearer pass and provide assistance (royal.uk). “Sovereignty is also a currency of international recognition” is the author's argument in thesis §2.2.
  22. 2:06 We are a nation—so we should have our own state.
  23. 2:11 We have a state—so we can claim sovereignty.
  24. 2:15 We have sovereignty and recognition from other states—so we really are a nation-state.
  25. 2:21 Each pillar is vouched for by the other two. The author calls this a recursive legitimacy loop.
  26. 2:27 Choose one: which came first, the nation or the state?
  27. 2:34 Inside this loop, the question has no starting point. It circles every day, making it seem self-evident.
  28. 2:44 But some people do not have this page.
  29. 2:48 No state recognizes him as its national. Such a person is stateless.
  30. 2:54 The loop creates not only an “us,” but also people it keeps outside.
  31. 2:59 In two episodes, we will explore why there is no “us” without “them.”
