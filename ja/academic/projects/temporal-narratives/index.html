Envisioning What Cannot Be Remembered · 6:57

Puzzle & Research Questions

以下は英語原稿の本文です。

Regional institutions may codify a shared past and an envisioned future together as the ground of their self-recognition. ASEAN continuously envisions its self-image in the future by publishing successive visions, from Vision 2020 to Community Vision 2025 to Community Vision 2045, whose strategic plans assign planned actions to particular sectoral bodies with timelines and review mechanisms (Association of Southeast Asian Nations [ASEAN], 1997, 2015, 2025). Yet an imbalance appears in the materials out of which it builds that image from past memories: although the Narrative of ASEAN Identity adopted in 2020 (ASEAN, 2020) does set out ASEAN's identity heritage in history, this proposal reads those shared memories as normative rather than event-based. By event-based I mean a past in which subjects are identified, responsibility is attributed, and collective judgement follows, where the conduct in question is that of a member government, toward another member or toward its own people. Whereas the EU grounds its "never again" future in a codified memory of the wars its member states once fought against one another (De Vries, 2020), the Konfrontasi of 1963 to 1966 was absorbed into principles rather than recorded as an event with named parties and attributed responsibility (ASEAN, 1967, 1976b; Jones, 2012).

Therefore, here lies the empirical puzzle: why does ASEAN codify its envisioned future in terms of specific prospective events and actors, while its shared memories remain in abstract terms? Research on national biography holds that envisioned futures are grounded in historical memories in developing knowledge of the "self" (Berenskoetter, 2014), and Bachleitner (2023) treats collective memory of significant historical others as a carrier of ontological continuity. Both presume a past available to be codified. A theoretical question thus runs parallel to the empirical one: under what conditions do event-based memories become difficult to institutionalize as a ground of self-image, and how does an entity consolidate its self-recognition under such an imbalance?
