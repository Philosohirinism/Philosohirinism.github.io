English introduction

Pregnancy and long-term care are separated to examine what is biological and what is assigned through the institution of motherhood.

英文字幕全文
  1. 0:01 Pregnancy takes about forty weeks.
  2. 0:03 Raising a child takes at least eighteen years.
  3. 0:05 Those forty weeks involve her body. Who assigned her the eighteen years?
  4. 0:10 Let's unpack the word “motherhood.”
  5. 0:12 Pregnancy, childbirth and breastfeeding do involve a particular body.
  6. 0:15 Their pain and risks are real. They should not be dismissed.
  7. 0:19 But what comes next?
  8. 0:21 Soothing a baby at night, changing diapers, tracking vaccines, attending school meetings.
  9. 0:24 The child has a fever: take leave. The child starts school: quit work. Which of these
  10. 0:30 requires a uterus?
  11. 0:32 Not one.
  12. 0:33 Yet they are bundled into one word:
  13. 0:35 motherhood. What ties the bundle together is not biology, but a sentence:
  14. 0:40 “You're the mother.”
  15. 0:41 Some say women are naturally better at childcare. The evidence? Usually, that women have always done it.
  16. 0:47 That uses the outcome to prove the cause.
  17. 0:49 A 2014 Israeli study found that fathers who were primary caregivers showed activity in brain regions linked to emotional vigilance close to that of primary-caregiving mothers. The more hands-on care fathers provided, the stronger the connections between relevant brain regions.
  18. 1:05 One study cannot settle this. But it raises a question: how much of what we call “instinct”
  19. 1:09 is skill acquired by those who started first?
  20. 1:12 At this point, we must mention someone.
  21. 1:15 In 1970, in The Dialectic of Sex, Shulamith Firestone went further than
  22. 1:19 anyone else. She did not represent all radical feminists,
  23. 1:23 but she pushed the question to one of its deepest points. She saw the division of reproductive labor itself as the root of women's oppression.
  24. 1:30 She imagined using technology, including artificial wombs, to free women from childbearing, then making both reproduction and childrearing society's responsibility.
  25. 1:38 That is radical. But in one respect, not radical enough. She placed the root in the uterus.
  26. 1:43 But if the problem is the uterus, why are mothers also assigned those eighteen years that require no uterus? What oppresses women is not “she can give birth.” It is society then signing on her behalf:
  27. 1:54 “Since you can give birth, everything else
  28. 1:56 comes bundled with it, and is yours too.”
  29. 1:58 Even if artificial wombs became widespread tomorrow, as long as “you're the mother” remains,
  30. 2:03 the same person would likely still get up at night, take leave and quit work.
  31. 2:08 Technology may remove those forty weeks, but not those eighteen years.
  32. 2:12 And sharing those eighteen years does not require waiting for technology. We can start today.
  33. 2:16 So the truly radical question is not how to escape childbearing. It is:
  34. 2:20 Why should giving birth become an IOU?
  35. 2:23 It reads: you gave birth, so you owe. Your time, your career, a lifetime of being on call.
  36. 2:32 She did not sign it. The signatories are marriage's default division of labor, “good mother” standards that judge only mothers, and workplaces assuming someone at home will always pick up the slack.
  37. 2:41 This does not mean wanting to be a mother is wrong.
  38. 2:44 The poet Adrienne Rich distinguished two kinds of motherhood:
  39. 2:48 one is the possible relationship between women and children;
  40. 2:51 the other is an institution that keeps that possibility, and all women, under male control.
  41. 2:59 We want to dismantle the second.
  42. 3:01 The desire to have children deserves respect. A life mortgaged to that desire
  43. 3:06 still deserves to be questioned.
  44. 3:07 So next time you hear “a mother should,” first ask:
  45. 3:12 Does this task require a uterus?
  46. 3:13 And if it does not,
  47. 3:15 why should it be the mother's duty?
  48. 3:18 The uterus accounts for forty weeks. Whoever assigned the remaining eighteen years should answer for that arrangement.
  49. 3:24 Next time, we ask:
  50. 3:25 when someone says, “I chose this freely,”
  51. 3:28 should the discussion end?
  52. 3:29 This is UjiBricks. See you next time.
