First, Learn Their Language

The episode asks what happens when seeking recognition requires speaking through an existing order’s terms.

English transcript
  1. 0:01 Previously: she had a card she wrote herself, but it still had no place in the club.
  2. 0:07 This time: what exactly does ‘no place’ mean? What does recognition cost?
  3. 0:16 Episode 6: You Must First Learn the Other Side’s Language
  4. 0:22 Turn over any assigned card in the club. On the back are four boxes, already filled in.
  5. 0:32 Who, when, how to interact with others, and where one ranks in the hierarchy. This is a ‘position.’
  6. 0:39 On the card she wrote herself, all four boxes are empty. It is not poorly written. Only the order can fill in those boxes.
  7. 0:47 Author’s OSIPP draft, 2B(i): an assigned identity already carries its position and relations within the order—‘who, when and how to interact, and in what hierarchy it is situated.’ The self-image created by a dissenting group lacks these.
  8. 0:56 So she is caught between two sides.
  9. 1:00 Reject more, and the card is more like her—but the club does not recognize it.
  10. 1:06 Reject less, and the club recognizes it—but it is no longer the self she wants to be.
  11. 1:11 The dissenting group’s anxiety: making its self-image satisfying (rejecting part of the assigned identity), while also making it recognized (not rejecting too much).
  12. 1:21 Author’s OSIPP draft, 2B(i): ‘anxious about making the self-image satisfying (by rejecting part of the assigned identity) while also making it recognized (by not rejecting too much).’
  13. 1:29 The first time she approaches the president, she says: ‘I want to dance my own dance.’
  14. 1:34 The president says: ‘There is no such item on the roster.’ It is not unreasonableness; the roster’s boxes have no word for it.
  15. 1:43 The second time, she puts it differently: ‘The club’s style × new elements—a fusion piece that could score points in a competition.’
  16. 1:50 The same dance, described in the club’s own words, now has a place on the roster.
  17. 2:01 To be recognized, a radical self-image must be translated into an acceptable agenda—using the order’s own grammar.
  18. 2:10 Author’s OSIPP draft, 2B(i): dissenting groups translate fairly radical self-images into acceptable agendas, seeking recognition through the political order’s grammar (the author cites Ferree 2003, pp. 339–340).
  19. 2:15 This is also an intuition the author brings to gender studies: often, those criticizing an order must first borrow its language to make their criticism heard.
  20. 2:25 The author’s intuition
  21. 2:30 Pause for a moment: have you ever changed how you put something so you could be heard? After changing it, was it still what you wanted to say?
  22. 2:40 Guardrail 1: translation does not mean surrender. You can borrow the other side’s language to say things it does not want to hear.
  23. 2:47 Guardrail 2: this is no one’s fault. The order draws the boxes. Anyone seeking a place on the roster must first learn how to write within them.
  24. 2:57 This episode’s comparison card (take a screenshot)
  25. 3:00 Position — Four boxes on the card’s back. Who, when, how to interact, and where one ranks.
  26. 3:04 No position — A self-written card with four empty boxes. Only the order can fill them in.
  27. 3:08 Anxiety in both directions — Reject too much: no recognition. Too little: it is not me. It must be satisfying and recognized.
  28. 3:14 Translation — The ‘fusion piece’ gets on the roster. Speaking one’s own words through the order’s grammar.
  29. 3:22 She is on the roster.
  30. 3:24 But which card made it onto the roster?
  31. 3:28 The chair is still there.

  33. 3:34 Did Those Who Created the Anxiety Relieve It?
