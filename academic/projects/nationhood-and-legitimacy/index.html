The Legitimacy That Died Twice

A Postmodern Dissection of Nationhood, Identity, and the Desire to Be Needed

Nationhood, identity, and the structural crisis of political legitimacy under postmodern conditions.

Abstract

This paper interrogates the structural crisis of legitimacy within the modern triadic formation of nation, state, and sovereignty under the conditions of postmodernity. Departing from classical theories of national identity (Anderson, 1983; Gellner, 1983), this work reconceptualizes “nation” not as an empirical social collectivity but as a symbolic apparatus contingent upon metaphysical certainties rooted in patriarchal order and epistemic stability. Drawing from Foucault’s (1980) power-knowledge nexus and Butler’s (1997) critique of subjectivation, the study unpacks how the performativity of identity has rendered the once-coherent legitimacy chain obsolete in the face of increasing ontological uncertainty and symbolic saturation.

Through a structural-theoretical analysis, the paper introduces the concept of the Transient Legitimacy Polity, building on Bleiker’s (2000) “transversal dissent” to describe postmodern formations that claim political legitimacy via affective marginalization rather than institutional representation. However, such formations remain symbolically parasitic on the very nation-logic they seek to transcend, producing a recursive deadlock of legitimacy.

Finally, the paper offers a speculative turn: moving beyond the symbolic death of nationhood, it proposes a shift toward embodied legitimacy, where subjectivity is anchored not in representational visibility but in intimate, situated relations of being needed and being loved. This “post-symbolic” model rejects both foundational identities and endless performativity, suggesting that the future of political legitimacy may reside not in being seen, but in being embedded.
