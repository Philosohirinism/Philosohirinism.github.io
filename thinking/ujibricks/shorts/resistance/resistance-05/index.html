English transcript
  1. 0:01 Previously: order has no heart, but it has many hands. What it fears is refusal.
  2. 0:08 This time, we take a step back: why would someone refuse an identity assigned to them?
  3. 0:17 Episode 5: Assigned Identity
  4. 0:23 Back to the street dance club. On the day the new member arrives, she receives a ready-made card.
  5. 0:29 The card says: new member—stand in the back row, practice the basics first, and dance in the club’s style. Who, where, and how to act are all written for her.
  6. 0:38 This card was not written just for her. It describes what a ‘new member’ should be—a social narrative.
  7. 0:48 But her own experience is different: she has practiced another kind of dance since childhood, for eight years, and performed onstage many times.
  8. 0:56 The card tells a social narrative; what she has lived is personal experience. They do not match, and dissatisfaction grows in the gap.
  9. 1:04 The author’s proposition
  10. 1:09 The street dance club is just a small example. The author’s draft gives a weightier one:
  11. 1:14 A patriotic gay citizen living under anti-LGBT policies may become dissatisfied with the identity of ‘citizen.’
  12. 1:22 He has not stopped loving his country. The ‘citizen’ identity assigned to him leaves no place for someone like him.
  13. 1:30 Author’s OSIPP draft, 2B(i): ‘A patriotic gay citizen under anti-LGBT policies may become dissatisfied with national identity.’
  14. 1:36 Resentment at being misrecognized makes her want to reject the card.
  15. 1:42 But people become ‘someone’ through social recognition. Throwing away the card will not make her freer—it will only make her invisible to others.
  16. 1:52 Author’s OSIPP draft, 2B(i): subjects are constituted through social recognition (the author cites Butler, The Psychic Life of Power, 1997, pp. 20–21), so they are compelled to seek an alternative identity, both for self-understanding and for recognition.
  17. 2:00 So she can only do something else: draw a card of her own.
  18. 2:06 ‘Someone who knows another dance.’ ‘Someone who wants to choreograph her own.’ A few others who also feel misunderstood gather around this card.
  19. 2:14 A dissenting group: creating a satisfying self-image in order to reject an identity that makes its members feel misunderstood.
  20. 2:25 Pause for a moment: is there an identity others have given you that has always felt like ‘That is not me’?
  21. 2:35 Guardrail 1: dissatisfaction does not mean being right. An assigned identity is not necessarily wrong; a self-image is not necessarily right.
  22. 2:41 Guardrail 2: where do the cards come from? This series argues that the material people use to understand themselves is itself regulated and produced by power.
  23. 2:50 Author’s OSIPP draft, 2B(i): the material subjects use for self-understanding is regulated and produced by power (the author cites Butler 1997, pp. 2–3). Here, ‘power’ means the dynamic form of the political order as a whole.
  24. 2:57 This episode’s comparison card (take a screenshot)
  25. 3:00 Assigned identity — ‘New member: stand in the back row; dance in the club’s style.’ A social narrative.
  26. 3:05 Where dissatisfaction comes from — The card versus her eight years of dancing. Social narrative does not match personal experience.
  27. 3:10 She cannot simply throw it away — Without the card, others cannot see her. People are constituted through recognition.
  28. 3:14 Dissenting group — The card ‘we wrote ourselves.’ Creating a satisfying self-image.
  29. 3:24 She now has a card she is satisfied with.
  30. 3:27 The problem is: this card still has no place in the club.
  31. 3:32 The chair is still there.
  32. 3:37 Next time:
  33. 3:38 To be recognized, you must first learn the other side’s language.
  34. 3:47 This is UjiBricks.
